# Slop rules: what makes site copy and design read as unreviewed AI output

Research track C, 2026-11-20. Feeds the anti-slop validator for gabrielbulacio.com.

The site is written with AI help and says so. That changes the target. The question is never "was AI involved?" (it was, and a detector would say so). The question is "would a CFO, a head of data or a technical reviewer conclude nobody checked this?" Every rule below targets a concrete quality defect that hurts trust whoever wrote it. Rules that only guess authorship are marked weak or left out.

Source IDs in brackets ([S1] and so on) point to section 4.

## How to read the severity column

- **block**: objectively wrong on a public page whoever wrote it (chatbot residue, placeholders, unverifiable testimonials, scaffold leftovers), or a pattern the author has already banned in his own voice guide. The build fails.
- **warn**: a lead for the reviewer agent. Many hits are fine in context. Counts and densities matter more than single hits.

Evidence strength: **strong** = measured in a peer-reviewed or large corpus study; **moderate** = a curated practitioner list with many observations (Wikipedia AI Cleanup, vendor first-party docs) or a single solid study; **weak** = blog or anecdotal commentary.

## 2. Reviewer rules (human judgment, for the reviewer agent)

Each answer is yes or no. A "no" is a fail and needs a fix or a written reason.

| id | pass/fail question | why | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | Swap test: if a competitor's name replaced Gabriel's on this page, would any sentence stop being true? (Pass = several would.) | Interchangeable copy is the core of "workslop": it looks finished and carries no information. Buyers screen vendors out on weak content | S17, S18 |
| R2 | Does every service listed name a deliverable, a typical client situation or a past engagement, rather than a category ("data strategy")? | Generic service lists are the consulting version of significance inflation | S16, S18 |
| R3 | Within the first screen, can a CFO tell who this is, what he builds, for whom, and see one piece of proof (a number with its source, a named project or a link to working software)? | B2B readers come for specifics; marketese lowers usability and credibility | S16, seniority-pass |
| R4 | Can every outcome number be traced to how it was measured (baseline, sample, period, who measured it), and does it carry its denominator ("127 of 146")? If not measured, is it labelled as a projection? | Unsubstantiated performance claims are a legal and trust risk; this is also the author's own voice rule | S19, voice.md |
| R5 | Is every testimonial from a real, named person with role and organization, quoted with permission, unedited in substance? Is every logo a real engagement the client agreed to show? | The FTC rule bans fake or AI-generated testimonials and undisclosed insider reviews; civil penalties apply per violation | S19 |
| R6 | Does each case study show a decision: the problem, what was chosen, at least one alternative rejected and why, known limits, and how the result was checked? | Experts who spot AI text cite lack of originality and formulaic structure; a visible decision is what reads as authored | S7, S17, seniority-pass |
| R7 | Where AI is mentioned as a capability, does the copy say which model or method, what it does, and how it is evaluated (eval set, accuracy, failure handling)? | The bare term "AI" lowers emotional trust and purchase intent, more so for high-risk purchases such as finance | S14 |
| R8 | Does the "How this was built" note say plainly what AI did and what the author decided and checked, without apology or hype, and is it consistent with the repo? | Disclosure costs some trust, but exposure by a third party costs more. A short, specific note that foregrounds human decisions is the least-bad option and also the honest one | S15b, seniority-pass |
| R9 | Does the last paragraph or section add something (a next step, a limit, a decision) instead of restating the intro? | Closing restatement is a listed tell and wastes the reader's last attention | S4 |
| R10 | Would this layout look different if made for a different consultant? Do colour, icons and cards each have a stated purpose (in docs/style-guide.md)? | AI-generated UIs converge on the statistical middle of web design | S21, S22 |
| R11 | Are visuals real work (screenshots of the demo, actual charts from the data) rather than illustrations, stock icons or abstract gradients? | Real artifacts are evidence; decoration is not | seniority-pass, S23 |
| R12 | For every contrast ("X, not Y"), is Y something a reader might actually believe, and does the contrast draw a precise line? | voice.md uses this form on purpose; the slop version knocks down a claim nobody made | voice.md, S4, S10 |
| R13 | Is every list of three a real list of three, with no padding item added for rhythm? | Rule of three makes thin analysis look complete | S4, S7 |
| R14 | Is the English idiomatic for a native business reader, with no Spanish false friends (actually/actualmente, eventually/eventualmente, assist/asistir, sensible/sensible, realize/realizar, "since years", "the same" as a pronoun, "in base to")? Fix them by hand, not by asking an LLM to "polish" the page. | Errors are not authenticity; a blanket LLM polish reintroduces L3 to L6 vocabulary, so the lint must run after every polish | S6, S8, section 3 |
| R15 | Does every claim about a client, dataset or result match what the repo and the client allow to be said (no confidential names, no invented scale)? | Inventing evidence is the one failure that cannot be fixed later | seniority-pass |

## 3. Detector reliability and the non-native writer

**Why the validator targets quality tells, not "is it AI".**

- OpenAI withdrew its own AI-text classifier on 20 July 2023 "due to its low rate of accuracy". At launch it caught 26% of AI text and wrongly flagged 9% of human text, and it was unreliable under 1,000 characters [S9]. Most website blocks are shorter than that.
- Liang et al. (2023) ran seven detectors on 91 TOEFL essays by non-native writers: average false-positive rate 61.22%; 89 of 91 were flagged by at least one detector, while US eighth-grade essays were classified almost perfectly. The cause was low perplexity: plainer vocabulary looks "machine-like". When ChatGPT rewrote the essays with fancier wording, detectors called them human [S6].
- Newer evidence is more mixed. A 2026 study on Czech found no systematic bias in current detectors [S8b], and Pangram reports very low false-positive rates on ESL sets (vendor data; one independent working paper found it the only tool meeting a strict false-positive cap) [S8c]. So "all detectors are biased" is no longer a safe claim. It does not matter here: this site is openly AI-assisted, so a detector would be right to flag parts of it and the result would say nothing about quality.
- Humans are mixed too. People who rarely use LLMs do badly and are overconfident; heavy LLM users, voting together, misclassified 1 of 300 articles. Their cues were vocabulary, sentence structure, originality, formulaic quotes and lists of exactly three [S7]. Business buyers in 2026 increasingly are heavy LLM users (84% of surveyed B2B decision makers used AI to summarize vendor content [S18]), so the tells in sections 1 and 2 are what they notice.
- Wikipedia's own guide warns that its signs are symptoms, that fixing surface markers without fixing the substance only makes text harder to judge, and that perfect grammar, formal prose and transition words are not reliable indicators [S4].

**Implications for a non-native author (Argentine Spanish first language).**

1. Do not treat plain, simple English as a defect. Low perplexity is what got non-native writers falsely flagged [S6]. Short declarative sentences with numbers are the target style (voice.md), not a risk.
2. Never add errors or "messiness" to look human. seniority-pass already forbids it, and Wikipedia lists mixed register and imperfect grammar as unreliable signals anyway [S4].
3. The real risk runs the other way: asking an LLM to "make my English better" is the step that injects the strong-signal vocabulary (Liang's rewrite experiment shows the polish changes word choice enough to flip detectors) [S6]. Run the lint after every AI polish pass, and prefer targeted fixes (R14) over whole-page rewrites.
4. Formal transitions ("Furthermore", "Moreover") and avoiding repeated words are taught in ESL and in Romance-language schools; Wikipedia notes repetition avoidance by non-native editors explicitly [S4]. Keep L9 at warn and never block on it.
5. Spanish uses the raya (long dash) for asides, so some dash use may be the author's own habit. In English copy the T2 limits still apply, because language models are a known high-dash source [S11, S12].
6. Encoding matters on Windows: agentic.md found slop_lint.py read files as cp1252, so em dashes and emoji passed silently. The validator must open files with `encoding="utf-8"` and fail on decode errors.

## 4. What the existing tooling gets right, misses, and gets wrong

Compared: the private case kit, the private case kit, a seniority-pass checklist and `scan.py`.

**Supported by the sources.**
- Vocabulary: delve, pivotal, crucial, testament, showcase, landscape, tapestry, realm, "serves as / stands as", "in today's", "it's important to note", "in conclusion" [S1 to S4, S7]. The scan.py and slop_lint lists overlap the evidence well for 2023 to 2025 models.
- Em-dash chains (slop_lint: 2+ per line; scan.py: density per 1k words). Supported, and still relevant because language models overuse them [S11, S12]; scan.py's density approach is the better design.
- "Not just X but Y" and "It's not X, it's Y" [S4, S10]. Emoji, bold-label bullets, Title Case, rule of three, closing summaries (seniority-pass lists all of them) [S4].
- Purple gradients, Inter-everywhere, three-card grids, generic hero (seniority-pass) [S21, S22]. Evidence is practitioner-grade, not experimental.
- voice.md's "counts carry their denominator" and "caveats sit beside the number" match the substantiation logic of S19 and the trust findings of S15 to S18. This is the most valuable rule in the set and no script enforces it yet.

**Missing (add these).**
- L1 chatbot residue, L2 placeholders, D1 scaffold leftovers: the cheapest blocks with zero ambiguity.
- L3 significance inflation and T4 participle tails: the strongest structural evidence (S5 is a PNAS corpus study) and neither script checks them.
- T12/T13 numbers without source or denominator, and R4/R5 for outcome numbers, testimonials and logos. Highest business risk, and FTC-relevant.
- L7 superlatives, L8 vague quantifiers, L10 AI buzzwords, L11 generic service lists, T5 question headings, T8 hedge stacks, T6 "Here's the thing" style signposts beyond the few scan.py has.
- Cross-sentence contrast ("isn't about X. It's about Y."): both regexes stay inside one clause and miss it.
- Newer-model vocabulary (L6) as density only; both lists stop at roughly GPT-4o.
- UTF-8 handling in slop_lint (already noted in agentic.md).

**False-positive traps.**
- `robust`, `leverage`, `comprehensive` are in the block lists. For a data consultant writing to a CFO these are normal technical or finance words ("robust standard errors", "financial leverage", "comprehensive income"). Downgrade to warn and allow them when followed by a technical noun.
- `crucial`, `journey`, `landscape`, `navigate the`, `harness`, `furthermore`, `moreover` as hard blocks in slop_lint: too blunt; Wikipedia rates transitions as ineffective indicators. Warn and use density.
- slop_lint's exclamation rule fires on `!=`, `!==`, markdown images and shell lines (T14).
- scan.py flags Inter on sight (D8) and `animate-pulse` everywhere (D10, shadcn Skeleton). It also flags `border-left: 4px`, which is the default way to style a blockquote.
- A naive "X, not Y" regex would block the author's own sanctioned contrast style (voice.md pattern 2). Only the framed versions in T1 should match.
- Curly quotes (T15) and the absence of em dashes are both unreliable after 2026 model changes [S4].

**Overall quality flags.**
- Lexical evidence is strong but comes from academic and encyclopedic corpora and from 2023 to 2025 models. Word lists decay as models change (delve fell sharply in 2025 [S4]); review L4 and L6 every six months against the Wikipedia page.
- No peer-reviewed rate study was found for "not X, it's Y". The rule rests on curated observation and press counts.
- All design and UI evidence is practitioner commentary plus one vendor statement. Keep every D rule except D1, D5 and D11 at warn.
- Consulting-buyer evidence is indirect: workplace surveys (S15), consumer experiments (S14), sponsor surveys (S18). The best-grounded consulting rules are the legal ones (S19) and the number-substantiation rules, which hold whatever the research says about AI.
